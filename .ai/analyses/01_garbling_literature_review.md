# Garbling in information economics: a comprehensive survey

**Garbling — the state-independent stochastic degradation of signals — serves as the central organizing concept connecting Blackwell's comparison of experiments, Bayesian persuasion, cheap talk, mechanism design, and rational inattention.** Since Blackwell's foundational theorem established that one information structure dominates another if and only if the latter can be obtained by garbling the former, the concept has become the principal formal tool for modeling how information is processed, distorted, and strategically designed across economic theory. This survey traces garbling from its measure-theoretic origins through its modern applications in information design, competitive persuasion, and dynamic disclosure, covering formal definitions, key theorems, and the full arc of the literature from 1951 to 2025.

---

## 1. Blackwell's theorem and the formal architecture of garbling

The modern theory of information comparison begins with two papers by David Blackwell: "Comparison of Experiments" (*Proceedings of the Second Berkeley Symposium on Mathematical Statistics and Probability*, 1951) and "Equivalent Comparisons of Experiments" (*Annals of Mathematical Statistics*, 1953). Blackwell defined an *experiment* (equivalently, *information structure* or *signal structure*) as a tuple $\pi = (\Omega, S, \{\pi(\cdot|\omega)\}_{\omega \in \Omega})$, where $\Omega$ is a finite state space, $S$ is a signal space, and $\pi(\cdot|\omega) \in \Delta(S)$ gives the conditional distribution of signals given state $\omega$. In the finite case, the experiment is represented by a **likelihood matrix** $L$ with entries $L_{\omega,s} = \pi(s|\omega)$, each row summing to one.

**The formal definition of garbling** captures stochastic degradation that is independent of the underlying state. Signal structure $\pi'$ is a *garbling* of $\pi$ if there exists a Markov kernel $K: S \to \Delta(S')$ such that for all states $\omega \in \Omega$ and all measurable sets $B \subseteq S'$:

$$\pi'(B|\omega) = \int_S K(B|s)\, \pi(ds|\omega).$$

The critical property is that $K$ does not depend on $\omega$. In the finite case, $K$ is an $|S| \times |S'|$ row-stochastic matrix, and the garbling condition reduces to the matrix equation $L' = L \cdot K^\top$. Equivalently, using random variables: $\pi'$ is a garbling of $\pi$ if the signal $s'$ can be generated as $s' = f(s, r)$ for some measurable function $f$ and random variable $r$ independent of $\omega$. This formulation makes precise the intuition that garbling adds noise unrelated to the state.

**Blackwell's sufficiency theorem** establishes the equivalence of four characterizations. For two information structures $\pi$ and $\pi'$ sharing the same state space:

**(a) Decision-theoretic dominance:** For every decision problem $(A, u, p)$ — action set, utility function, prior — the optimal expected payoff under $\pi$ weakly exceeds that under $\pi'$: $V(\pi, D) \geq V(\pi', D)$.

**(b) Garbling:** There exists a state-independent Markov kernel $K$ such that $\pi'(\cdot|\omega) = K \circ \pi(\cdot|\omega)$ for all $\omega$.

**(c) Feasibility inclusion:** The set of implementable state-contingent action distributions under $\pi$ contains that under $\pi'$.

**(d) Convex function ordering on posteriors:** For every continuous convex function $v: \Delta(\Omega) \to \mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{E}[v(q^\pi)] \geq \mathbb{E}[v(q^{\pi'})]$. Equivalently, the distribution of posteriors under $\pi$ is a **mean-preserving spread** of the distribution under $\pi'$.

The equivalence of (a) and (d) follows from the convexity of indirect utility functions. The deep content lies in proving the equivalence of (a) and (b). When $\pi' $ is obtainable from $\pi$ by garbling, we write $\pi \succeq_B \pi'$, defining the **Blackwell order** — a partial order on experiments. The 1951 paper treated the finite case; the general measure-theoretic extension was completed by the **Blackwell-Sherman-Stein theorem** (Sherman 1951; Stein 1951). The definitive monograph treatment is Torgersen's *Comparison of Statistical Experiments* (Cambridge University Press, 1991).

The concept connects directly to **sufficient statistics** in mathematical statistics. Fisher's notion of sufficiency (1920/1922) — $T(X)$ is sufficient for $\theta$ if the conditional distribution of $X$ given $T(X)$ does not depend on $\theta$ — is a special case: the garbling from full data to a sufficient statistic is the conditional distribution of data given the statistic. The Halmos-Savage factorization theorem (1949, *Annals of Mathematical Statistics*) provides the measure-theoretic foundation. Marschak and Miyasawa ("Economic Comparability of Information Systems," *International Economic Review*, 1968) introduced the term "garbling" into the economics literature, grounding Blackwell's statistical framework in economic decision theory. Crémer ("A Simple Proof of Blackwell's 'Comparison of Experiments' Theorem," *Journal of Economic Theory*, 1982) provided an economically intuitive proof.

## 2. Bayesian persuasion and the optimal design of garbling

Kamenica and Gentzkow's "Bayesian Persuasion" (*American Economic Review*, 2011) recast information design as the problem of choosing an optimal garbling. A sender selects a signal structure $\pi$ to influence a receiver who observes the signal realization, updates beliefs via Bayes' rule, and takes an action maximizing her own utility. The sender's choice of $\pi$ is equivalent to choosing how much to garble the fully informative signal — the state itself.

The key reformulation works through **distributions over posteriors**. A distribution $\tau \in \Delta(\Delta(\Omega))$ is **Bayes-plausible** if $\mathbb{E}_{\mu \sim \tau}[\mu] = \mu_0$ (the expected posterior equals the prior). Kamenica and Gentzkow prove that every Bayes-plausible distribution can be induced by some signal, and the sender's problem reduces to:

$$V(\mu_0) = \max_{\tau \in \text{BP}(\mu_0)} \mathbb{E}_{\mu \sim \tau}[\hat{v}(\mu)]$$

where $\hat{v}(\mu) = \mathbb{E}_\mu[v(a^*(\mu), \omega)]$ is the sender's indirect utility over posterior beliefs. The solution is the **concavification** of $\hat{v}$: $V(\mu_0) = \text{cav}(\hat{v})(\mu_0)$, the value of the smallest concave function everywhere weakly above $\hat{v}$, evaluated at the prior. The sender benefits from persuasion if and only if $\text{cav}(\hat{v})(\mu_0) > \hat{v}(\mu_0)$ — that is, when the value function is not already concave at the prior.

This concavification technique was first developed by Aumann, Maschler, and Stearns in classified reports for the US Arms Control and Disarmament Agency (1966–68), studying repeated zero-sum games of incomplete information, published as *Repeated Games with Incomplete Information* (MIT Press, 1995). The connection is structural: an informed player's optimal long-run strategy in a repeated game involves the same mathematics as a Bayesian persuader's optimal disclosure policy.

**When is partial garbling optimal?** No disclosure is optimal when $\hat{v}$ is concave (the sender prefers the receiver to remain uninformed). Full disclosure is optimal when $\hat{v}$ is convex. The most interesting case — partial garbling — arises when $\hat{v}$ is neither globally concave nor convex. There always exists an optimal signal using at most $|\Omega|$ signal realizations. In Kamenica and Gentzkow's prosecutorial example with $\Pr(\text{guilty}) = 0.3$ and conviction threshold at $\mu = 0.5$, the optimal signal achieves a **60%** conviction rate by splitting the prior into posterior masses at $\mu = 0$ (with probability 0.4) and $\mu = 0.5$ (with probability 0.6).

The connection between garbling and mean-preserving contractions is formalized by Gentzkow and Kamenica ("A Rothschild-Stiglitz Approach to Bayesian Persuasion," *American Economic Review*, 2016). They map each signal $\pi$ to a convex function $c_\pi(x) = \int_0^x G_\pi(t)\,dt$, where $G_\pi$ is the CDF of posterior means. A signal $\pi_1$ is a garbling of $\pi_2$ if and only if $c_{\pi_1} \leq c_{\pi_2}$. The set of feasible signals is characterized by convex functions sandwiched between the uninformative and fully informative bounds — providing a complete geometric characterization of the garbling order in terms of posterior distributions.

Dworczak and Martini ("The Simple Economics of Optimal Persuasion," *Journal of Political Economy*, 2019) establish a price-theoretic approach: when sender utility depends only on the expected state, the problem reduces to choosing a distribution of posterior means that is a mean-preserving contraction of the prior, and duality yields a verification theorem — a distribution is optimal if and only if there exists a convex price function weakly above the sender's utility that touches it on the support. Rayo and Segal ("Optimal Information Disclosure," *Journal of Political Economy*, 2010) independently developed the optimal disclosure framework, characterizing "bait and switch" strategies as forms of optimal garbling.

Kolotilin ("Optimal Information Disclosure: A Linear Programming Approach," *Theoretical Economics*, 2018) provides LP methods for settings with privately informed receivers and binary actions. Kolotilin, Mylovanov, Zapechelnyuk, and Li ("Persuasion of a Privately Informed Receiver," *Econometrica*, 2017) show that in linear environments with log-concave type densities, the optimal mechanism takes an **upper censorship** form — revealing the state below a threshold and pooling above. More recent work by Arieli, Babichenko, Smorodinsky, and Yamashita ("Optimal Persuasion via Bi-Pooling," *Theoretical Economics*, 2023) and Dworczak and Kolotilin ("The Persuasion Duality," *Theoretical Economics*, 2024) has further refined the analytical toolkit.

## 3. The Bergemann-Morris framework for information design in games

Bergemann and Morris generalize Bayesian persuasion from single-receiver settings to games with multiple strategic players. Their "Bayes Correlated Equilibrium and the Comparison of Information Structures in Games" (*Theoretical Economics*, 2016) introduces **Bayes Correlated Equilibrium (BCE)** as the solution concept. A decision rule $\sigma: \Theta \times T \to \Delta(A)$ mapping states and signal profiles to action distributions is a BCE if it satisfies **obedience constraints** — each player prefers to follow the recommendation given her signal. The revelation principle for information design states that the set of BCE outcomes characterizes all possible Bayesian Nash equilibrium outcomes across all information structures.

The relationship between garbling and implementable outcomes is fundamental: **more garbled signals lead to larger BCE sets**, because coarser information imposes fewer incentive constraints. If $S'$ is a garbling of $S$, then $\text{BCE}(G, S) \subseteq \text{BCE}(G, S')$. The designer's problem reduces to optimizing over a convex polytope defined by linear obedience constraints — a linear program when the objective is linear.

Their comprehensive survey "Information Design: A Unified Perspective" (*Journal of Economic Literature*, 2019) unifies Bayesian persuasion, correlated equilibrium (Aumann 1974, 1987), and Myerson's communication framework, noting that selective garbling is often optimal: the designer reveals some dimensions of the state while concealing others. Bergemann and Morris define an **individual sufficiency ordering** on information structures: $S$ is individually sufficient for $S'$ if each player's signal in $S$ is a sufficient statistic for her signal in $S'$.

Mathevet, Perego, and Taneva ("On Information Design in Games," *Journal of Political Economy*, 2020) develop the **belief-based approach** — the multi-agent analog of Kamenica-Gentzkow's concavification. They decompose general information design into a **within** step (choosing optimal private information for each common belief) and a **between** step (concavifying the resulting value function over public beliefs). Taneva ("Information Design," *American Economic Journal: Microeconomics*, 2019) provides computational methods showing that conditionally independent private signals are generically suboptimal — correlation among signals benefits the designer.

## 4. Cheap talk produces garbling endogenously through strategic incentives

Crawford and Sobel's "Strategic Information Transmission" (*Econometrica*, 1982) established the foundational model of strategic communication in which garbling arises endogenously. A sender observes state $\theta \sim \text{Uniform}[0,1]$ and sends a costless message to a receiver. In the canonical uniform-quadratic specification, $U^R = -(y-\theta)^2$ and $U^S = -(y-\theta-b)^2$, where $b > 0$ measures the sender's systematic upward bias.

Every equilibrium is a **partition equilibrium**: the state space is partitioned into $N$ intervals $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_N = 1$, and the sender reveals only which interval contains $\theta$. The partition boundaries satisfy the difference equation $t_{i+1} - t_i = (t_i - t_{i-1}) + 4b$ — successive intervals grow by exactly $4b$, reflecting the sender's incentive to exaggerate. The maximum number of equilibrium partition elements is $N^*(b) = \lfloor -\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{1 + 2/b}\rfloor$. As bias increases, $N^*$ decreases — the garbling becomes coarser. The **babbling equilibrium** ($N=1$) always exists and represents maximal garbling: zero information transmission. Under a monotonicity condition, the most informative equilibrium ($N = N^*$) is ex ante Pareto-superior. Chen, Kartik, and Sobel ("Selecting Cheap-Talk Equilibria," *Econometrica*, 2008) introduced the NITS criterion that selects this equilibrium.

This partition structure is formally a garbling of the identity signal. The sender possesses perfect information but transmits only a coarsened signal through an equilibrium garbling channel. The critical distinction from Bayesian persuasion is that **cheap talk garbling arises from strategic incentives rather than by design** — the sender cannot commit to a communication strategy, and the partition is sustained by self-enforcing incentive constraints.

Lipnowski and Ravid ("Cheap Talk with Transparent Motives," *Econometrica*, 2020) bridge the two frameworks elegantly. When sender preferences are state-independent, the value of cheap talk is characterized by the **quasiconcave envelope** of the sender's value function — strictly below the concave envelope (the Bayesian persuasion value). The restriction from concavification to quasiconcavification precisely captures the incentive constraint of cheap talk: only "flat" belief splittings (where all posteriors yield equal sender payoff) are sustainable, whereas Bayesian persuasion allows arbitrary affine splittings.

Several extensions enrich the cheap talk framework. Krishna and Morgan ("A Model of Expertise," *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2001) show that with two experts biased in opposite directions, consulting both strictly improves information transmission — opposing biases partially unravel garbling. Battaglini ("Multiple Referrals and Multidimensional Cheap Talk," *Econometrica*, 2002) demonstrates that with multidimensional states, **full revelation is generically possible** with two or more senders regardless of bias magnitude, though Ambrus and Takahashi (*Theoretical Economics*, 2008) show this relies on unbounded state spaces.

Dessein ("Authority and Communication in Organizations," *Review of Economic Studies*, 2002) establishes that delegation dominates communication whenever informative communication is feasible, because the information loss from partition garbling can exceed the control loss from delegation. Kartik ("Strategic Communication with Lying Costs," *Review of Economic Studies*, 2009) spans the spectrum from cheap talk to verifiable disclosure by introducing lying costs, showing how the nature of garbling varies continuously with the cost parameter.

## 5. Exogenous noise and mediation as instrumental garbling

Blume, Board, and Kawamura ("Noisy Talk," *Theoretical Economics*, 2007) introduce exogenous noise into the Crawford-Sobel framework, discovering the counterintuitive result that **noise can improve welfare**. With quadratic preferences and uniform types, a sufficiently small amount of exogenous garbling raises welfare for almost every bias level. The mechanism operates through plausible deniability: when the receiver knows messages may be corrupted, she attributes less meaning to extreme messages, reducing the sender's incentive to exaggerate and enabling finer partition equilibria. There exists a noise level achieving the optimal welfare obtainable by any communication device — the bound characterized by Goltsman, Hörner, Pavlov, and Squintani ("Mediation, Arbitration and Negotiation," *Journal of Economic Theory*, 2009).

Goltsman et al. compare negotiation (face-to-face cheap talk), mediation (non-binding recommendations from a neutral party), and arbitration (binding third-party decisions). A **mediator explicitly implements a garbling channel**: the sender reports to the mediator, who transmits a randomized recommendation to the receiver. The optimal mediation rule garbles the sender's report to weaken the link between message and response, relaxing incentive constraints. Unmediated negotiation matches mediation performance only when conflict is low; for high bias, mediation strictly dominates. Myerson's general framework (*Game Theory: Analysis of Conflict*, 1991) establishes that any equilibrium with a mediator can be represented as a communication equilibrium where the mediator's strategy is a garbling of players' reports.

## 6. Competition in persuasion and the conditions for information unraveling

Gentzkow and Kamenica ("Competition in Persuasion," *Review of Economic Studies*, 2017) analyze simultaneous signal choice by multiple senders. The central condition governing competition's effect is **Blackwell-connectedness** — whether any two feasible signals are Blackwell-ordered. Under Blackwell-connectedness (equivalently, rich signal spaces where senders can choose arbitrarily informative experiments), three strong results hold for any preferences: every equilibrium outcome is at least as informative as every collusive outcome; adding senders cannot decrease informativeness; and decreasing alignment of sender preferences cannot decrease informativeness.

The equilibrium characterization is elegant: a distribution of posteriors $\tau$ is an equilibrium outcome if and only if every posterior $\mu$ in its support satisfies $\text{cav}(\hat{v}_i)(\mu) = \hat{v}_i(\mu)$ for every sender $i$ — no single sender can profitably split any equilibrium belief. This draws on Aumann-Maschler methods. The **unilateral deviation lemma** from their companion paper (*Games and Economic Behavior*, 2017) captures the fundamental asymmetry: a sender can always add information but cannot remove information already generated by other senders.

Without Blackwell-connectedness, competition's effect is ambiguous. Li and Norman ("On Bayesian Persuasion with Multiple Senders," *Economics Letters*, 2018) construct examples where adding senders **reduces** information when signals are not arbitrarily correlatable, when senders move sequentially, or when mixed strategies are permitted. Their "Sequential Persuasion" (*Theoretical Economics*, 2021) characterizes sequential persuasion equilibria via **iterated concavification**, showing that sequential persuasion is weakly less informative than simultaneous and strictly less so with binary states. Adding a sender who moves first cannot reduce informativeness, but adding a sender in the middle or end may.

Au and Kawai ("Competitive Information Disclosure by Multiple Senders," *Games and Economic Behavior*, 2020) study symmetric senders each endowed with independent projects. As the number of senders $N$ increases, each discloses more aggressively; in the limit, full disclosure arises. Board and Lu ("Competitive Information Disclosure in Search Markets," *Journal of Political Economy*, 2018) show that the competitive effect depends critically on observability: with private buyer beliefs, full information emerges as search costs vanish, but with observable beliefs, a Diamond-like result allows sellers to sustain monopoly-level garbling.

## 7. Multiple receivers and the constraint of audience diversity

When signals must reach multiple audiences, garbling design becomes substantially more complex. Alonso and Câmara ("Persuading Voters," *American Economic Review*, 2016) study a politician designing a public signal to influence voters under a $k$-voting rule. With a single voter, the standard concavification applies. With multiple voters and non-unanimous rules, the win set (belief profiles leading to adoption) is generally **not convex**, but persuading with the win set is payoff-equivalent to persuading with its convex hull. The optimal experiment exploits voter heterogeneity by targeting different winning coalitions with different signal realizations. Voters prefer supermajority rules because these force the politician to supply more informative signals.

Bardhi and Guo ("Modes of Persuasion Toward Unanimous Consent," *Theoretical Economics*, 2018) distinguish **general persuasion** (each voter's signal can depend on all states) from **individual persuasion** (each voter's signal depends only on her own state). Under general persuasion, the sender makes the most demanding voters indifferent. Under individual persuasion, voters are optimally partitioned into rubber-stampers, fully informed voters, and partially informed voters. General persuasion weakly dominates individual persuasion because correlating signals across voters provides strictly more flexibility.

Arieli and Babichenko ("Private Bayesian Persuasion," *Journal of Economic Theory*, 2019) study private correlated signals to multiple receivers in a binary state space. For **supermodular** sender utilities, the optimal policy correlates positive recommendations maximally conditional on the bad state. For general submodular utilities, computing the optimum is NP-hard (Dughmi and Xu, 2017). Public signals are optimal only when the sender's utility is additive across receivers. Chan, Gupta, Li, and Wang ("Pivotal Persuasion," *Journal of Economic Theory*, 2019) show that private persuasion strictly dominates public persuasion when receivers are heterogeneous, exploiting the pivotal voter mechanism.

The fundamental trade-off in multi-receiver garbling is that a public signal well-calibrated for one audience may be poorly calibrated for another, creating a multi-dimensional concavification problem whose geometry depends on the voting rule, voter heterogeneity, and state correlation structure.

## 8. Garbling as mediation in mechanism design

The connection between garbling and mechanism design runs deep. Myerson ("Optimal Coordination Mechanisms in Generalized Principal-Agent Problems," *Journal of Mathematical Economics*, 1982) formalized how a mediator receives type reports and sends randomized recommendations — the recommendation mapping is a garbling of agents' reported types. The revelation principle states that without loss of generality, the principal restricts attention to direct incentive-compatible mechanisms where agents truthfully report and follow recommendations. In "Multistage Games with Communication" (*Econometrica*, 1986), Myerson extended this to dynamic settings, defining **communication equilibria** as outcomes achievable through sequential communication with a mediator, where the mediator's mapping at each stage is a garbling of accumulated reports.

Forges ("An Approach to Communication Equilibria," *Econometrica*, 1986) introduced three solution concepts of increasing generality: correlated equilibrium, extensive-form correlated equilibrium, and communication equilibrium. Each has a canonical representation via the revelation principle, and each corresponds to a different class of garbling the mediator may perform. In "Five Legitimate Definitions of Correlated Equilibrium in Games with Incomplete Information" (*Theory and Decision*, 1993; corrected 2006), Forges showed that different garbling classes — non-communicating versus coordinated — generate different equilibrium concepts.

Aumann's correlated equilibrium (1974, *Journal of Mathematical Economics*; 1987, *Econometrica*) is the prototypical case of mediated garbling: a correlation device observes a random state and privately tells each player a recommended action. Lehrer, Rosenberg, and Shmaya ("Garbling of Signals and Outcome Equivalence," *Games and Economic Behavior*, 2013) characterize when two information structures are equivalent under different solution concepts, proving that under Nash equilibrium, equivalence holds if and only if the structures are independent garblings of each other.

As Kamenica ("Bayesian Persuasion and Information Design," *Annual Review of Economics*, 2019) notes, information design and mechanism design are mirror images: in mechanism design, the information allocation is given and the designer selects the game; in information design, the game is given and the designer selects the garbling.

## 9. Partial orders on information structures beyond the Blackwell order

The Blackwell order is coarse — most experiments are incomparable. Several weaker orderings extend its reach to specific decision environments.

**Lehmann ordering** (Lehmann, "Comparing Location Experiments," *Annals of Statistics*, 1988) restricts attention to monotone decision problems with single-crossing payoffs. Experiment $E$ is more Lehmann-accurate than $F$ if every decision-maker with a single-crossing payoff prefers $E$. Blackwell dominance implies Lehmann dominance but not conversely. Kim ("Comparing Information in General Monotone Decision Problems," *Journal of Economic Theory*, 2023) provides a garbling-based interpretation via **monotone quasi-garbling** — a modification where added noise is state-dependent but "reversely monotone."

**Persico** ("Information Acquisition in Auctions," *Econometrica*, 2000) applied Lehmann's ordering to auction theory with affiliated signals. **Athey and Levin** ("The Value of Information in Monotone Decision Problems," *Research in Economics*, 2018; first circulated 1998) provide necessary and sufficient conditions for information comparisons across specific payoff classes: supermodular payoffs require stochastic dominance of posteriors; single-crossing payoffs recover Lehmann's ordering; affine payoffs reduce to mean comparisons; quadratic payoffs to mean-variance ordering.

The **mean-preserving spread** characterization (condition (d) of Blackwell's theorem) connects directly to Rothschild and Stiglitz ("Increasing Risk: I. A Definition," *Journal of Economic Theory*, 1970). If $\pi'$ is a garbling of $\pi$, the posterior distribution under $\pi'$ is a mean-preserving contraction of that under $\pi$, with both satisfying Bayesian plausibility. This equivalence undergirds the entire Bayesian persuasion framework.

**Supermodular orderings** (Shaked and Shanthikumar, 1997; Müller and Scarsini, 2000; Meyer and Strulovici, 2013) capture increasing interdependence between signals and states rather than overall informativeness. The comprehensive treatment of stochastic orders appears in Müller and Stoyan's *Comparison Methods for Stochastic Models and Risks* (Wiley, 2002) and Shaked and Shanthikumar's *Stochastic Orders* (Springer, 2007).

**Le Cam's deficiency** (Le Cam, "Sufficiency and Approximate Sufficiency," *Annals of Mathematical Statistics*, 1964) quantifies how far one experiment is from dominating another: $\delta(E, F) = \inf_T \sup_\theta \|Q_\theta - TP_\theta\|$, where the infimum is over Markov kernels. Zero deficiency corresponds to Blackwell dominance, and the Le Cam distance $\Delta(E,F) = \max\{\delta(E,F), \delta(F,E)\}$ defines a pseudometric on experiments. Raginsky ("Shannon Meets Blackwell and Le Cam," IEEE ISIT, 2011) unified this with information-theoretic channels.

## 10. Garbling in applied economic settings

The garbling framework has been deployed across numerous applied domains, each illustrating how the abstract theory translates into concrete institutional design.

**Stress tests and financial regulation.** Goldstein and Leitner ("Stress Tests and Information Disclosure," *Journal of Economic Theory*, 2018) model a regulator designing an optimal disclosure policy about bank quality. When expected quality is high, no disclosure (full garbling) preserves risk-sharing by avoiding the Hirshleifer effect. When expected quality is low, some disclosure is necessary; the optimal rule often takes a **pass/fail cutoff** form. Inostroza and Pavan ("Adversarial Coordination and Public Information Design," *Theoretical Economics*, 2025) study stress test design in global games, showing the optimal public signal removes strategic uncertainty while preserving heterogeneity in structural uncertainty — a nuanced form of garbling that manages higher-order beliefs.

**Certification and auditing.** Lizzeri ("Information Revelation and Certification Intermediaries," *RAND Journal of Economics*, 1999) proves that a monopoly certifier optimally reveals only whether quality exceeds a minimum standard — binary garbling (pass/fail) — capturing a large share of surplus despite minimal information transmission. Competition among certifiers drives toward full revelation. DeMarzo, Kremer, and Skrzypacz ("Test Design and Minimum Standards," *American Economic Review*, 2019) characterize optimal test design as a garbling problem, and Perez-Richet and Skreta ("Test Design Under Falsification," *Econometrica*, 2022) extend the analysis to settings where agents can falsify outcomes.

**Grading and education.** Ostrovsky and Schwarz ("Information Disclosure and Unraveling in Matching Markets," *American Economic Journal: Microeconomics*, 2010) show that schools choose grading policies (garblings of student ability) that balance the benefits of pooling high- and low-ability students. Boleslavsky and Cotton ("Grading Standards and Education Quality," *American Economic Journal: Microeconomics*, 2015) demonstrate the surprising result that grade inflation (garbling) can be welfare-enhancing: when evaluators respond to strategic grading by relying more on school reputation, schools invest more in education quality.

**Media bias.** Gentzkow and Shapiro ("Media Bias and Reputation," *Journal of Political Economy*, 2006) model media bias as strategic garbling: firms slant reports toward consumer priors to build reputation for accuracy, implementing a stochastic mapping from observed signals to published reports. Mullainathan and Shleifer ("The Market for News," *American Economic Review*, 2005) show that under duopoly, firms differentiate by becoming more extreme than the most extreme consumers — a Hotelling differentiation in garbling space — but consumers who cross-check reports can become better informed than under monopoly.

## 11. Rational inattention reframes garbling as an endogenous choice

Sims ("Implications of Rational Inattention," *Journal of Monetary Economics*, 2003) introduced the framework in which agents have limited information-processing capacity measured by Shannon mutual information. The agent chooses an optimal **self-garbling** of available information subject to a capacity constraint $\kappa$. The noise is endogenous: the nature of garbling depends on the dynamics of the state being tracked and the available capacity.

Matějka and McKay ("Rational Inattention to Discrete Choices: A New Foundation for the Multinomial Logit Model," *American Economic Review*, 2015) showed that under Shannon entropy costs, the rationally inattentive agent's optimal garbling produces choice probabilities following the **multinomial logit** form: $P(a_i|\omega) \propto \exp(u_i(\omega)/\lambda) \times \text{prior}(a_i)$, where $\lambda$ is the cost parameter. The agent freely chooses the conditional distribution of signals given states — the garbling — subject to entropy cost, making the posterior uncertainty endogenously determined.

Hébert and Woodford ("Neighborhood-Based Information Costs," *American Economic Review*, 2021) derive a richer class of **uniformly posterior-separable** cost functions that capture perceptual distance: distinguishing nearby states is costlier than distinguishing distant ones. Unlike mutual information, these costs accurately predict perceptual discrimination experiments. Caplin and Dean ("Revealed Preference, Rational Inattention, and Costly Information Acquisition," *American Economic Review*, 2015) develop behavioral axioms characterizing when observed choice data is consistent with rational inattention models. Maćkowiak, Matějka, and Wiederholt provide the comprehensive survey ("Rational Inattention: A Review," *Journal of Economic Literature*, 2023).

## 12. The axiomatic cost of reducing garbling

Pomatto, Strack, and Tamuz ("The Cost of Information: The Case of Constant Marginal Costs," *American Economic Review*, 2023) develop an axiomatic theory of information acquisition costs. Three axioms — **Blackwell monotonicity** (more informative signals cost more), **additivity** (the cost of two independent signals equals the sum), and **mixture linearity** — pin down the cost function up to a vector of parameters, yielding the **log-likelihood ratio cost**: $C(\pi) = \sum_{i \neq j} \beta_{ij} \cdot \mathbb{E}[\log(\pi(s|\omega_i)/\pi(s|\omega_j))]$, where $\beta_{ij}$ captures the difficulty of distinguishing states $i$ and $j$.

Their framework connects to the **data processing inequality**: if $\theta \to S \to S'$ is Markov (i.e., $S'$ is a garbling of $S$), then $I(\theta; S) \geq I(\theta; S')$ — garbling cannot increase mutual information. This holds for Shannon mutual information, Rényi divergences, $f$-divergences, and KL divergence. However, as Mu, Pomatto, and Tamuz ("From Blackwell Dominance in Large Samples to Rényi Divergences and Back Again," 2021) show, mutual information alone does not characterize the Blackwell order — the complete characterization requires all Rényi divergences simultaneously.

Mu et al. prove that strict Rényi dominance implies dominance in large samples: if $R^\theta_\mu(t) > R^\theta_\eta(t)$ for all $t \in (0, \infty]$ and all $\theta$, then $\mu^{\otimes n} \succeq_B \eta^{\otimes n}$ for all large $n$. They further show that the extreme rays of the cone of additive, Blackwell-monotone cost functions are precisely the Rényi divergences. Bloedel and Zhong (2024) characterize cost functions robust to sequential experimentation, while Bordoli and Iijima (2025) extend the framework using Max-Rényi costs.

## 13. Dynamic information design and frontier research

Dynamic settings introduce temporal dimensions to garbling. Ely ("Beeps," *American Economic Review*, 2017) studies a principal who privately observes a stochastic process and sends messages ("beeps") to an agent over time, characterizing the optimal dynamic garbling. Ely, Frankel, and Kamenica ("Suspense and Surprise," *Journal of Political Economy*, 2015) model dynamic information revelation as choosing an optimal **belief martingale** — a sequence of garblings over time — where suspense equals the variance of next-period beliefs and surprise equals the distance between current and prior beliefs. Renault, Solan, and Vieille ("Optimal Dynamic Information Provision," *Games and Economic Behavior*, 2017) characterize optimal dynamic persuasion using martingale theory. Koessler, Laclau, Renault, and Tomala ("Long Information Design," *Theoretical Economics*, 2022) study information design in long-run interactions.

**Robustness** has emerged as a major theme. Dworczak and Pavan ("Preparing for the Worst but Hoping for the Best: Robust (Bayesian) Persuasion," *Econometrica*, 2022) study maximin-optimal garbling when the sender is uncertain about the receiver's exogenous information and equilibrium selection. Their key finding: **robustness generally calls for more disclosure** (less garbling) than standard Bayesian persuasion, because the worst-case scenario of adverse coordination by the receiver makes opacity dangerous. Kosterina ("Persuasion with Unknown Beliefs," *Theoretical Economics*, forthcoming) provides conditions for robustness across families of receiver priors.

Recent work by Kolotilin and Zapechelnyuk ("Persuasion Meets Delegation," *Econometrica*, 2025) and Koessler and Skreta ("Informed Information Design," *Journal of Political Economy*, 2023) continues to push the frontier, connecting garbling design to signaling theory and delegation. Fréchette, Lizzeri, and Perego ("Rules and Commitment in Communication: An Experimental Analysis," *Econometrica*, 2022) provide experimental evidence on whether commitment to garbling rules improves outcomes in practice.

## Conclusion: garbling as the connective tissue of information economics

The garbling concept provides the connective tissue unifying disparate branches of information economics. Blackwell's 1951 insight — that stochastic degradation independent of the state is the right notion of information reduction — has proven remarkably durable and generative. The formal architecture is simple: a Markov kernel $K$ that does not depend on $\omega$, transforming one likelihood matrix into another. Yet this simple structure organizes a vast literature.

Three key intellectual developments stand out. First, the shift from garbling as a partial order (Blackwell's comparison question: which experiment is "better"?) to garbling as a design variable (Kamenica-Gentzkow's persuasion question: which garbling is optimal?). Second, the recognition that garbling arises endogenously through strategic incentives (Crawford-Sobel) and through cognitive constraints (Sims), not only through explicit design. Third, the discovery that competition among senders generally drives toward less garbling under rich signal spaces (Gentzkow-Kamenica 2017), while robustness concerns also push toward greater transparency (Dworczak-Pavan 2022) — suggesting that the economic forces acting on garbling in complex environments tend toward information revelation rather than concealment.

The frontier of research continues to expand along several dimensions: dynamic garbling over time, axiomatic foundations for the cost of information acquisition, robust design under model uncertainty, and computational methods for multi-agent information design. The concept of garbling — mathematically clean, economically fundamental — remains at the center of how economists think about the creation, destruction, and strategic manipulation of information.
